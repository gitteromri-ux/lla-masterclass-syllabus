The thinking behind every slide

Built from her Version C script, every spoken paragraph, and from her own 15 October notes. One slide, one idea, one object. Her headlines, word for word. Nothing is on screen that she is not saying at that moment.

Her own rules for the deck, from the script: one idea, one headline, one picture per slide. No bullet lists; where there is a list, the object is the list. Numbers are the largest thing on screen. The navy field is lit, never flat. Every slide carries one hero object or one full-bleed photograph. The cake returns at the start of each layer with that layer lit.
SLIDE 1 · 2:00

Welcome. I am very normal.

WHAT SHE IS SAYING

An ad promised something impressive and now the room is looking at a normal woman. She is normal. That is the whole point. Second in the world in 2023, on about twelve dollars a day, while the people around her on the list spent small fortunes. She is an engineer; she became the scientist and the experiment.

THE ONE IDEA

A normal person, an extraordinary number, almost no money. The whole hour lives in the gap between those two facts.

ON SCREEN, AND WHY

She is the picture. The slide must not compete with her face, so there is no object. A navy field lit from behind, and then the two facts arrive one after the other, as large as anything in the deck: "Second in the world, 2023" in cream, "About twelve dollars a day" in light blue. Nothing else. The restraint is the design: the room reads the two numbers, looks back at her, and the contradiction does the work her words are doing.

BUILT WITH

Typography only. Two builds. The light behind the field is the only motion.

SLIDE 2 · 2:00

Why I went down this path.

WHAT SHE IS SAYING

Nobody wakes up wanting to be on a leaderboard. She is a single mom; for most of her son's life it has been the two of them. She wants to be the mother who can still carry the groceries at seventy and still hold a conversation at ninety. She watched someone she loves age faster than they should, and the question stopped being "will it happen to me" and became "how fast, and what can I do."

THE ONE IDEA

Her why has a face. This is the only slide in the hour that is allowed to be personal before it is scientific.

ON SCREEN, AND WHY

Her script asks for one photograph, full bleed, graded into the navy, headline low and left, nothing moving. That photograph is hers to give: her and her son, or her alone. A generated picture here would be a lie in the one place the hour cannot afford one. Until she sends it, the slide carries her two ages instead: 70 and 90 as the two largest numbers on screen, with her two lines beneath them, "carry the groceries" and "hold a conversation". The numbers make the why concrete without inventing a face.

BUILT WITH

Her photograph when it arrives. Until then, the two ages in Playfair, cream and light blue, on the lit field.

SLIDE 3 · 1:30

Ten years pass. By this test, I age about six. The speed towards death.

WHAT SHE IS SAYING

For every ten calendar years her body has aged about six, by this test. Most people age ten. The test is DunedinPACE; scientists call it pace of aging, she calls it the speed towards death because it is the same thing and harder to ignore. 1.0 means one year per year. Her best was 0.65, her three-test average 0.665, and the honest part the ads leave out: it went back up to 0.76 and she is testing again in December.

THE ONE IDEA

Everyone in the room is moving toward the same place; the only question is how fast. She found the speedometer.

ON SCREEN, AND WHY

Three builds, in her order. First the plain line alone, huge, so the room gets the point before any science. Then the speedometer she drew herself: a dark glass instrument, 1.0 at the top labelled "most people", the needle on 0.665 in light blue, the small tick at 0.76, the right side of the dial running into red toward "faster than the calendar". Every number is engraved into the face of the instrument, part of the object, not laid over a photo. Third, the population curve rises beside it: most people bunched at 1.0, a tail aging fast, and one marked point at 0.665. The gauge says what she measures; the curve says where she sits among everyone else. Both are needed, because the speed means nothing without the crowd.

The instrument is built as a real object in code, so the labels are part of the dial: brushed rim, dark glass, engraved ticks, a glowing needle. The curve is drawn, with her point lit. DunedinPACE, Belsky et al., eLife 2022; Balachandran et al., Nature Aging 2024.

SLIDE 4 · 1:30

Longevity is like a cake.

WHAT SHE IS SAYING

Aging is the biggest risk factor for the four things everyone fears: heart disease, cancer, dementia, type 2 diabetes. Slow the aging itself and all four move back at once; that is geroscience, and that is her bet. Then her picture: the cake is four things, sleep, food, movement, stress management, where most of the work lives. One more thin layer for the women in the room. The icing is sauna and the expensive therapies. Nobody has ever eaten a bowl of icing and called it a cake.

THE ONE IDEA

Four layers do the work. The icing is on top for a reason: it comes last and it holds nothing up.

ON SCREEN, AND WHY

First build, small and plain: one root, four branches. Aging at the root, the four diseases on the branches. Second build: the cake, whole, hero size, every layer lit evenly this once, labelled exactly as she drew it: Sleep at the bottom, Food, Movement, Stress Management, the thin layer beneath them, the icing labelled Therapies. It is a real cake, baked and iced, because she is a baker and the metaphor only works if the room wants to eat it. It returns at the start of every layer with that layer lit, so the cake becomes the map of the whole hour.

BUILT WITH

The whole uncut six-layer cake, photographed; the layer labels sit beside it with leader lines, not on it. The geroscience tree is drawn. Buck Institute; López-Otín et al., Cell 2023.

SLIDE 5 · 5:30

Not wrong. Not first.

WHAT SHE IS SAYING

She starts with the icing because it is everywhere and it makes people feel behind. She is not saying don't; she is saying not first. The whole spectrum on one slide: affordable on the left, sauna, prebiotics, most supplements; real money on the right, NAD drips, peptides, stem cells; rapamycin, a prescription and a doctor; and at the far end the two-million-a-year program. Nobody in the blue zones is in a hyperbaric chamber. Four things she would leave alone until the cake is baked. Her own icing: the sauna, three to five days a week, the one piece of icing with twenty-eight years of data behind it, and the reason she gets out of bed. And the coin at the far left: twelve dollars a day, mostly groceries.

THE ONE IDEA

Price and proof are two different axes. The expensive end of the room is not where the evidence is.

ON SCREEN, AND WHY

A map, not a list: cost left to right, strength of human evidence bottom to top. Each therapy is a marker placed where it belongs, so the room sees in one glance that sauna sits cheap and high, the drips and peptides sit expensive and low, rapamycin sits in the middle with a doctor beside it, and the two-million program is off the edge. Four markers turn amber when she names them. The sauna marker warms when she tells the sauna story. The coin lands last, bottom left. The map carries the whole argument of the block: order, not prohibition.

BUILT WITH

SLIDE 6 · 2:30

Menopause therapy is a layer, not icing.

WHAT SHE IS SAYING

One thing gets filed under icing by mistake, usually by people who have never been through it. For half the room, menopause changes sleep, bones, mood and body, and it changes how well every other layer can do its job. Decoration does not do that; this is a thin layer of the cake. The Menopause Society's 2022 position: for healthy symptomatic women under sixty or within ten years of menopause, benefits generally outweigh risks. In November 2025 the FDA removed the black-box warnings. The action is one line: find a doctor through the Menopause Society. Then the bridge: four layers hold up the cake.

THE ONE IDEA

This belongs in the cake, not on it. And there is a window.

ON SCREEN, AND WHY

The cake returns with everything dark except the thin layer, lit. That is the entire first build: the room sees the layer she means. Second build: a short timeline, menopause to ten years after, with the window highlighted, because the science she quotes is a window and the slide should look like one. The line beneath: The Menopause Society, find a certified practitioner. Final build: the four layers light bottom to top, the icing dims, and the headline changes to "Four layers hold up the cake", which is the sentence that starts the next forty-five minutes.

SLIDE 7 · 4:00

Sleep is the repair shift.

WHAT SHE IS SAYING

Sleep is when the body does its maintenance while she is not in the way, and that is not a metaphor: the brain's drainage channels were imaged in living people for the first time in 2024. Regularity predicted who lived longer better than duration, in sixty-one thousand people; the most regular sleepers had up to a forty-eight percent lower risk of dying. Her exact numbers: lights out at nine, up at five, last meal three hours before bed, screens off an hour before, cool and dark. Borrow the order, not her clock.

THE ONE IDEA

Night is a shift with a schedule, and the same bedtime every day matters more than the hours.

ON SCREEN, AND WHY

The cake with sleep lit, for her first sentence. Then the night arc: 9 PM on the left, 5 AM on the right, moon to sun, one arc of light across a dark sky, with a band across the deepest part of the night labelled "the repair shift". Her five numbers are the only type, placed along the arc where they happen: last meal at six, screens off at eight, lights out at nine, up at five, eight hours between. The arc turns her rules into a single shape the room can picture tonight at 8:45.

SLIDE 10 · 5:00

The one thing every diet agrees on.

WHAT SHE IS SAYING

Every diet disagrees except on this: sugar is bad for aging. The model in three words. Spike, what a glucose monitor shows after a sugary meal. Signal, what the spike switches on: insulin and the nutrient-sensing pathways, and when they are on all day that is one of the twelve hallmarks of aging. Scar, the part people don't know: sugar sticks to proteins, glycation, and HbA1c is literally sugar stuck to red blood cells. UCSF, 2024: every gram of added sugar came with an older biological age. BMJ: under 25 grams a day. Her confession: pizza barely moved her monitor; the healthy green drink spiked her.

THE ONE IDEA

Sugar is not a calorie problem. It spikes, it signals, and it scars, and you can measure all three.

ON SCREEN, AND WHY

One object, the glucose trace from a monitor like the one every Academy student wears: a sugary meal as the sharp spike, a balanced plate as the low wave beside it. The three words sit at the three moments of the object: Spike at the peak; Signal as the band beneath the curve that stays lit after the spike has fallen; Scar at the tail, where the HbA1c number lives. The trace is hers in spirit, the green drink that spiked her. Final build: the object dims and two large checkboxes appear, Move dinner earlier and Rebuild one plate, under "Your two Monday changes", because that is how the sleep and food half of the hour ends.

BUILT WITH

The trace drawn as a monitor would draw it, the signal band and the scar mark part of the chart. Chiu, Epel and Laraia, JAMA Network Open 2024; Huang et al., BMJ 2023; López-Otín et al., Cell 2023.

SLIDE 12 · 3:30

Four types of movement, four parts of you.

WHAT SHE IS SAYING

Movement is a portfolio; everything in one stock is not diversified, it is optimistic. Four tiles. Weights, resistance against a load, compound lifts: for bones and muscle. VO2 max, how much oxygen the body can use when working hard: for the heart. Zone 2, easy cardio at a conversational pace: for the heart. Mobility and balance, the tile everybody forgets: for the brain as much as the body, and the one that keeps you doing the other three. Each tile has a number researchers use to predict how long you live: grip strength, fitness, steps a day, the one-leg stand. Seven thousand steps, not ten. Try the one-leg stand tonight.

THE ONE IDEA

Four kinds of movement, each feeding a different part of you, each with a number that predicts your life.

ON SCREEN, AND WHY

The body map: one translucent figure in profile, realistic, not neon, with four points that light as she names them: bones and muscle, heart, heart, brain. Around the figure, four tiles, and each tile is a real picture of the movement, a woman under a bar, a woman on a bike at full effort, a woman walking at a talking pace, a woman standing on one leg, with a line from the tile to the part of the body it serves and the predictor number beside it: grip strength, fitness, 7,000 steps, the one-leg stand. SLIDE 14 · 5:00

The layer people skip.

WHAT SHE IS SAYING

Stress, the layer people skip, and the one she found out was real the hard way: a divorce, a test, cortisol high. Stress hormones are built for short bursts; when they never switch off they wear the body down. She found a transcendental meditation center, learned it, and six months later her cortisol was normal and stayed there. Not a promise; the best practice is the one you will do. Then the free tool: the physiological sigh, tested at Stanford on 111 people, five minutes a day for a month. Two short inhales through the nose, one long exhale through the mouth. Three together, on camera.

THE ONE IDEA

Stress is a number that can come down, and the cheapest tool for it takes thirty seconds.

ON SCREEN, AND WHY
